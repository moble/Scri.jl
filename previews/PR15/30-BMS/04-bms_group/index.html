The BMS Group

Bondi-Sachs coordinates, metric, and gauge

The natural setting for outgoing radiation is a coordinate system built from the light cones themselves. We foliate the neighborhood of $ℐ⁺$ by outgoing null cones and label each cone by a retarded time $u$ that is constant across it. We then establish coordinates on the cross-sections by a radial coordinate $r$ together with two angles $xᴬ = (θ, ϕ)$ that stay fixed along each outgoing null ray. That ray — the generator of the cone — is the null vector $ℓ$ of the standard tetrad. These are the Bondi–Sachs coordinates [6].

Bondi gauge is a set of conditions that restrict these coordinates — though they remain arbitrary up to BMS transformations. We take $r$ to be an areal radius, so that the determinant of the angular metric is $r⁴$ times that of the round unit sphere, and we require the metric to approach Minkowski as $r → ∞$, with its angular part approaching $r² dΩ²$ — the round unit-sphere metric. The full asymptotic expansion of the metric is standard [6] and thoroughly coordinate-dependent; we will not need it. We need only one fact drawn from it: asymptotically, the angular part of the metric is $r²$ times a round unit-sphere metric.

That condition is what gives the conformal factor its significance. An asymptotic symmetry is free to rescale $r$ and to change the round metric on the sphere, but Bondi gauge ties the two together: the sphere metric may change only by an overall, angle-dependent rescaling, and the rescaling of the radius against the rescaling of the sphere is precisely the conformal factor $κ$ (which Sachs [6] calls $K$; his equations 3.5 and 3.7). The rest of this page makes that statement precise by dropping the coordinates and keeping the geometry — the cross-sections of $ℐ⁺$ assemble into a celestial sphere, and the asymptotic symmetries act on it conformally.

Supertranslations $𝒮$

We stick with the approach implicit in the original papers, which is to define our transformations as acting passively. And in this case, the supertranslations are defined as transformations of the time coordinate. (Again, we can refer to either the retarded time $u$ or the advanced time $v$, so we will use the more neutral notation $t$.) So a supertranslation relates the time coordinate of a point $p$ in one frame to the time coordinate of the same point $p$ in another frame as

\[t'(p) = t(p) - c_α α(p),\]

where $p$ refers to a point on $ℐ$, and $α$ is a real-valued function that is independent of $t$. The $c_α$ is just a bookkeeping device to keep track of the differing conventions for the sign in the formula above.

BMS

We now have two groups: the (proper orthochronous) Lorentz group $ℒ$ and the supertranslations $𝒮$. The supertranslation $α ∈ 𝒮$ acts solely on the time coordinate as

\[t'(t, 𝐤) = t - c_α α(𝐤).\]

A Lorentz transformation, with frame rotor $\boldsymbol{Λ} ∈ ℒ$, changes the spatial coordinates as well as the time coordinate, the latter as

\[t'(t, 𝐤) = κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}, 𝐤) t,\]

where $κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}, 𝐤) = k⁰ / (\boldsymbol{Λ̃}\, 𝐤\, \boldsymbol{Λ})⁰$ is the ratio of the old to the new time component of the null ray $𝐤$, whose new components are those of $\boldsymbol{Λ̃}\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ}$ (see Frames and Components).

We can combine these two transformations, applying $α$ first and then $\boldsymbol{Λ}$, to get the general transformation of the time coordinate:

\[t'(t, 𝐤) = κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}, 𝐤) [t - c_α α(𝐤)].\]

Now, we can investigate how repeated transformations compose. Take $α₁, α₂ ∈ 𝒮$ and $\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, \boldsymbol{Λ}₂ ∈ ℒ$, and we apply $α₁$ followed by $\boldsymbol{Λ}₁$, and then $α₂$ followed by $\boldsymbol{Λ}₂$, where $α₂$ and $\boldsymbol{Λ}₂$ act on the frame that the first pair produces. As on Frames and Components, the basis $𝐞_μ$ changes first to $𝐞'_μ = \boldsymbol{Λ}₁\,𝐞_μ\,\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁$ and then to

\[𝐞''_μ = \boldsymbol{Λ}₂\, 𝐞'_μ\, \boldsymbol{Λ̃}₂ = \boldsymbol{Λ}₂\boldsymbol{Λ}₁\, 𝐞_μ\, \boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₂,\]

so the frame rotor of the two changes together is $\boldsymbol{Λ}₂\boldsymbol{Λ}₁$, with the later rotor on the left. The components of the null ray in the intermediate frame are those of

\[𝐤' = \boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\, 𝐤\, \boldsymbol{Λ}₁,\]

and its time components in the three frames are therefore $k⁰$, $k^{0'} = (\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)⁰$, and $k^{0''} = (\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₂\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ}₂\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)⁰$. From these, we can compute the cocycle property of $κ$:[1]

\[\begin{aligned} κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 𝐤) &= \frac{k⁰}{(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₂\, 𝐤\, \boldsymbol{Λ}₂\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)⁰} \\ &= \frac{(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\, 𝐤\, \boldsymbol{Λ}₁)⁰} {(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₂\, 𝐤\, \boldsymbol{Λ}₂\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)⁰}\, \frac{k⁰}{(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\, 𝐤\, \boldsymbol{Λ}₁)⁰} \\ &= \frac{k^{0'}}{k^{0''}}\, \frac{k⁰}{k^{0'}} \\ &= κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂, 𝐤)\,κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 𝐤). \end{aligned}\]

In the last step, we used the fact that $κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂, 𝐤)$ is the ratio of the time components of $𝐤$ before and after the second change of frame. Like any frame rotor, $\boldsymbol{Λ}₂$ is understood relative to the frame it acts from, which here is $𝐞'_μ$. In the code, where Λ₂ holds the components of $\boldsymbol{Λ}₂$ in that frame, this factor is computed as conformal_factor(Λ₂, k̂′), from the label $k̂'$ of the ray in that frame.

These two results allow us to write

\[\begin{aligned} t'' &= κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂, 𝐤) [t' - c_α α₂(𝐤')] \\ &= κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂, 𝐤) \left\{κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 𝐤) [t - c_α α₁(𝐤)] - c_α α₂(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁𝐤\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)\right\} \\ &= κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 𝐤) \left\{t - c_α [α₁(𝐤) + α₂(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁𝐤\boldsymbol{Λ}₁) / κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 𝐤)]\right\}. \end{aligned}\]

Here $α₂$ is evaluated at $𝐤'$ because it is a function of the labels of the rays in the intermediate frame. That is, the result of a sequence of two transformations is another transformation of the same form:

\[t''(t, 𝐤) = κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}, 𝐤) [t - c_α α(𝐤)],\]

where

\[\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{Λ} &= \boldsymbol{Λ}₂ \boldsymbol{Λ}₁, \\ α(𝐤) &= α₁(𝐤) + α₂(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁𝐤\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)/κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 𝐤). \end{aligned}\]

Note that $α₂$ is to be evaluated at the same geometric point on $ℐ$ as $α₁$ and $κ$ in this expression, but is presumably expressed with respect to the transformed frame — it is given as a function of the null rays in those transformed components.

We can formalize this a little more directly by forming the (outer) semidirect product [33, page 230] of these two groups:

\[\text{BMS} = ℒ ⋉_{\mathrm{Ad}} 𝒮,\]

where the homomorphism $\mathrm{Ad}: ℒ → \text{Aut}(𝒮)$ is given by

\[\mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ})(α)(𝐤) = \frac{α(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ})}{κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}, 𝐤)},\]

where $α(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ})$ means $α$ evaluated at the label of the ray whose components are those of $\boldsymbol{Λ̃}\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ}$.

The identity element of this group is the pair $(1, 0)$. We write products in the order in which their factors are applied, so that a general element, whose supertranslation is applied first, is

\[(\boldsymbol{Λ}, α) = (1, α)\,(\boldsymbol{Λ}, 0).\]

The group operation is then

\[(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, α₁)\,(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂, α₂) = \bigl(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂ \boldsymbol{Λ}₁, α₁ + \mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)(α₂)\bigr),\]

and the inverse is $(\boldsymbol{Λ}, α)^{-1} = \bigl(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}, -\mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ̃})(α) \bigr)$. Here each $\boldsymbol{Λ}$ is a frame rotor, and each element acts on the frame produced by the elements before it, so $(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂, α₂)$ acts on the frame produced by $(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, α₁)$. Note that the BMS product is written in the order of application, whereas the geometric product of frame rotors puts the later rotor on the left. The frame rotors therefore compose as $\boldsymbol{Λ}₂\boldsymbol{Λ}₁$, and their reverses, which give the new components, as $\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₂$ (see Frames and Components). In the code, where Λ₂ holds the components of $\boldsymbol{Λ}₂$ in the frame that $\boldsymbol{Λ}₁$ produces, the frame rotor of the composite is Λ₁ * Λ₂.

The name comes from conjugation. Writing each element as a product of its parts in the order of application, as above, gives

\[(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, α₁)\,(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂, α₂) = (1, α₁)\, \bigl[(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 0)\,(1, α₂)\,(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 0)^{-1}\bigr]\, (\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 0)\,(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂, 0),\]

and comparing this with the group law shows that $(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 0)\,(1, α₂)\,(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 0)^{-1} = \bigl(1, \mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)(α₂)\bigr)$. That is, $\mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ})$ is the action of $ℒ$ on $𝒮$ by conjugation, and it appears in the group law directly.

Composing with a Lorentz transformation and dividing by $κ$ preserves the defining properties of the supertranslations, so $\mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ})$ is indeed an automorphism. The cocycle property of $κ$ noted above then gives $\mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂ \boldsymbol{Λ}₁) = \mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)\, \mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂)$. The left side belongs to the change of frame by $\boldsymbol{Λ}₁$ followed by $\boldsymbol{Λ}₂$, which is their product in $ℒ$ when that product is also written in the order of application, so $\mathrm{Ad}$ is a homomorphism.

The groups $ℒ$ and $𝒮$ are isomorphic to subgroups of $\text{BMS}$ in the obvious way: we can identify any Lorentz transformation with the element $(\boldsymbol{Λ}, 0)$, and any supertranslation with the element $(1, α)$. We will abuse notation slightly and refer to the elements $\boldsymbol{Λ}$ and $α$ themselves as elements of the BMS group. Note that, by this construction, $𝒮$ is a normal subgroup of $\text{BMS}$, but $ℒ$ is not. That is, for $\boldsymbol{Λ} ∈ ℒ$ and $α ∈ 𝒮$, the element $\boldsymbol{Λ} α \boldsymbol{Λ}⁻¹$ is still an element of $𝒮$, but $α \boldsymbol{Λ} α⁻¹$ need not be an element of $ℒ$. This fact will be useful later.

We have essentially constructed the BMS group, but Sachs [6] actually derived it from the asymptotic metric conditions, and analyzed the group structure after the fact [21]. Sachs [6] was the first to describe the BMS group. (He referred to it as the "Generalized Bondi-Metzner group", or GBM group; later authors renamed it the Bondi-Metzner-Sachs group to honor his contribution.) His slightly later paper [21] was more specifically about the BMS group itself, and proved some important properties, including:

  1. The supertranslations form an abelian normal subgroup $N$ of the BMS group; the factor group is isomorphic to the Lorentz group.
  2. The translations form a normal four-dimensional subgroup of the proper BMS group.
  3. If $N'$ is a four dimensional normal subgroup of the proper BMS group then $N'$ is contained in the supertranslation group $N$.
  4. The only normal four dimensional subgroup of the BMS group is the translation group.

In particular, the Poincaré group — which Sachs refers to by its longer name, the inhomogeneous (proper) orthochronous Lorentz group — is a subgroup of the BMS group, though it is not a normal subgroup. Conjugating a translation by a Lorentz transformation gives another translation, but conjugating a Lorentz transformation by a proper supertranslation gives an element outside the Poincaré group, as shown on the geometric tetrad page.

Which way around is κ?

It is easy to confuse $κ$ with $1/κ$ in these formulas. Ours is the ratio $κ = k⁰/k^{0'}$ of the old to the new time component of a fixed null ray, whose new components are those of $\boldsymbol{Λ̃}\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ}$. Sachs [6] calls the same factor $K$, but he applies the supertranslation after the Lorentz transformation, on the new sphere, so his $α$ differs from ours by a factor of $κ$; the conversion is given on the page Frames and Components. A discerning consistency check is Poincaré closure. That is, we perform a transformation that comes from the Poincaré subgroup of BMS and check that the BMS result is the same as the simpler Poincaré result. Specifically, the time translation $α = δt$ corresponds to a spacetime translation by the 4-vector $\boldsymbol{δ} = (δt, 0, 0, 0)$. We can readily compute $\boldsymbol{Λ} \boldsymbol{δ} \boldsymbol{Λ̃} = γ\,δt\,(1; v⃗)$ for a pure boost with velocity $v⃗$, whose frame rotor is $\boldsymbol{Λ} = 𝐁(v⃗)$, which is equivalent to the BMS supertranslation $α' = γ\,δt\,(1 - v⃗⋅k̂) = δt/κ$.

Our group multiplication law is consistent, because we take $\boldsymbol{Λ}₁=\boldsymbol{Λ}$, $α₁=0$, $\boldsymbol{Λ}₂=\boldsymbol{Λ}⁻¹$, and $α₂=δt$ to find that

\[(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, α₁)\,(\boldsymbol{Λ}₂, α₂) = \boldsymbol{Λ}\,δt\,\boldsymbol{Λ}⁻¹ = \bigl(1, \mathrm{Ad}(\boldsymbol{Λ})(δt)\bigr) = (1, δt / κ).\]

The last equality is because $α$ is constant over the sphere, so evaluating in the direction $\boldsymbol{Λ̃}\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ}$ is the same as evaluating in the direction $𝐤$, and the resulting value is $δt$. That factor of $1/κ$ is given by our composition law, and is exactly the same as the result of the Poincaré transformation.

Representations of the supertranslation

Strictly speaking, there are two BMS groups: $\text{BMS}⁺$ acting on $ℐ⁺$ and $\text{BMS}⁻$ acting on $ℐ⁻$. They are isomorphic — canonically so, under the antipodal matching of "Future and past null infinity" [23, 24] — so nothing about the group structure distinguishes them; what differs is just the representation of elements. The supertranslation $α$ is stored as mode weights of a function of the labels $k̂$ on the celestial sphere, and the two ends of null infinity are labeled antipodally: at $ℐ⁺$ a generator is labeled by its outgoing propagation direction, while at $ℐ⁻$ it is labeled by the direction in which an observer sees it — opposite to the propagation. The same abstract supertranslation therefore has two mode representations, related by composition with the antipodal map $A: k̂ ↦ -k̂$:

\[α ↦ α ∘ A, \qquad (α ∘ A)_{ℓ,m} = (-1)^ℓ\, α_{ℓ,m},\]

since $Y_{ℓ,m}(-k̂) = (-1)^ℓ\, Y_{ℓ,m}(k̂)$. The Lorentz part needs no such choice — a rotor is representation-neutral — but its realization as a map of labels does depend on the labeling: on the antipodal labels, the label map $𝐤 ↦ \boldsymbol{Λ̃}\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ}$ becomes the conjugated map $A ∘ \boldsymbol{Λ̃} ∘ A$, which flips the boost part ($v⃗ ↦ -v⃗$) while leaving rotations alone — exactly why the conformal factor and the direction map involve the $ℐ$ sign.

The BMS type therefore retains that choice as data — the I type parameter — declaring which labeling the stored modes refer to, alongside the analogous supertranslation-sign convention A (the sign with which $α$ enters the time law $t' = κ(t - c_αα)$). Both default to +1: future null infinity, and the plus sign. The conversion constructor BMS(g; c_α, ℐ) (see BMS) converts an element between representations — exactly, since both conversions are pure sign flips.

Because the "twist" in the twisted group law,

\[α(𝐤) = α₁(𝐤) + α₂(\boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁𝐤\boldsymbol{Λ}₁)/κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 𝐤),\]

is built from the label map $𝐤 ↦ \boldsymbol{Λ̃}₁\,𝐤\,\boldsymbol{Λ}₁$ and the conformal factor $κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}₁, 𝐤)$ — both realized on the labeled sphere — the coordinates of a composite element depend on the representation even though the abstract group does not. To make that precise, write $·⁺$ and $·⁻$ for the two products: each is the formula above, with the label map and conformal factor computed from the corresponding section $𝐤 = (1, ±k̂)$. Note that $κ$ itself needs no such decoration: as defined on the null-cones page, it is a single function of a Lorentz transformation and a null vector — indeed, being scale-invariant in $𝐤$, a function of the null ray — and the two labelings merely feed it antipodal rays, $κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}, (1, -k̂))$ on $ℐ⁻$ whereas it is $κ(\boldsymbol{Λ}, (1, +k̂))$ on $ℐ⁺$. The same is true of the label map. Now we promote the relabeling of $α$ to a map on whole elements,

\[\begin{aligned} P \colon \text{BMS}⁺ &→ \text{BMS}⁻, \\ (\boldsymbol{Λ}, α) &↦ (\boldsymbol{Λ}, α ∘ A), \end{aligned}\]

which sends the coordinates of a $\text{BMS}⁺$ element to the coordinates of the matched $\text{BMS}⁻$ element — and, being an involution, back again. Then the two products are related by

\[P(g₁) ·⁻ P(g₂) = P(g₁ ·⁺ g₂).\]

That is, composing the matched elements at $ℐ⁻$ yields the matched composite — precisely the statement that $P$ is a group isomorphism $\text{BMS}⁺ → \text{BMS}⁻$. Pointwise, the matched element acts antipodally: if the point with old coordinates $(t, k̂)$ has new coordinates $(t', k̂')$ under $g$ on $ℐ⁺$, then the point with old coordinates $(t, -k̂)$ has new coordinates $(t', -k̂')$ under $P(g)$ on $ℐ⁻$.

In the code, all this bookkeeping is handled automatically. Operations that combine two elements (compose, ==, isapprox) accept any mix of conventions, accounting for the differences exactly; each convention the two inputs share is kept for the result, and each convention on which they disagree is resolved to the default +1. Single-element operations — the action functor, which maps new coordinates to old, conformal_factor, and inv — use the element's own conventions, and the transform! bridge re-represents the element to match the null infinity declared by its DataComponents argument.

Note that the $ℐ$ relevant to DataComponents is conceptually distinct from the $ℐ$ relevant to the BMS element. It may be quite reasonable, for example, to always consider the effect a BMS element has on future null infinity, but still be concerned with the transformations of data on past null infinity. This is the reason why both BMS and DataComponents each have their own ℐ parameter.

Decomposition of BMS

Essentially by our definition, we have already decomposed $\text{BMS}$ into the supertranslations and the Lorentz transformations. However, it can also be useful to further decompose these parts. Once we have chosen a frame $𝐭, 𝐱, 𝐲, 𝐳$ to work with, we can conventionally decompose any element of the BMS group as shown in the diagram below.

The supertranslations decompose naturally into "proper" supertranslations and ordinary spacetime translations — the latter of which further decompose into time translations $δt$ and space translations $δx⃗$. In general, the supertranslations are functions on the sphere, which we decompose into spherical harmonics. If the supertranslation is a pure spacetime translation, then the only nonzero spherical harmonic modes are the $ℓ=0$ mode for time translations and the $ℓ=1$ modes for space translations. Equivalently, we can think of such translations in terms of their physical parameters $δt$ and $δx⃗$.

The Lorentz transformations decompose naturally into rotations $𝐑$ and boosts $v⃗$. These are parameters of the new frame, whose frame rotor $\boldsymbol{Λ} = 𝐁(v⃗)\,𝐑$ is stored as part of the BMS element (see Frames and Components). It is already convenient to represent the rotation as a quaternion, which can be constructed in numerous ways, including the generator of the rotation, the axis and angle of the rotation, the Euler angles, or the spherical coordinates and spin angle (which is essentially a different version of Euler angles). Finally, a general boost may be decomposed into a 1-D boost of rapidity $φ$ in a given direction, and a null rotation of parameter $ξ$ about that direction. This is not often an intuitive decomposition, but it is important in discussions of boost weight.
