The gradient of the objective

One of the biggest challenges when comparing waveforms — whether from numerical simulations, or from surrogate, analytical (e.g., PN or EMRI), or quasi-analytical (e.g., EOB) models — is understanding how the gauge freedom affects the comparison. All waveforms have this intrinsic BMS freedom. One way to deal with this freedom is to optimize the BMS transformation that brings one waveform into the best agreement with another. The obvious measure to use for "best agreement" is the weighted $L^2$ difference between the two waveforms — integrated over both the sphere and time. We call this the objective function $Φ(θ)$, where $θ$ parameterize the BMS transformation, implemented as the function transform_objective. One crucial element in making this efficient is the fact that the objective function eliminates the need to compute the full transformed waveform; we don't need to transform back to modes if we just need to integrate the difference from the other waveform. But we can do better if we think specifically about the goal here.

To actually minimize this quantity efficiently, we don't just need its value, we also need its gradient with respect to the parameters of the BMS transformation, $∂Φ/∂θ^p$. There are six parameters for the Lorentz transformation and $(ℓ_α+1)^2$ more for a supertranslation with modes up to $ℓ_α$, so there are easily dozens of parameters, and the obvious ways of computing the gradient — finite differences, or dual numbers in every parameter — cost about one evaluation of $Φ$ for each parameter.

This page explains how objective_and_gradient! computes the value and gradient of the objective function with respect to all of the parameters efficiently, at the cost of only a few evaluations of the objective function itself — almost independently of the number of parameters. In brief, $Φ$ is computed by a chain of four simple steps, repeated for each direction on the sphere. The parameters enter that chain only through a small amount of geometry for each direction, which is cheap to compute but complicated. The data enter through sums over every mode and every time, which are expensive but simple. We differentiate the geometry forward, with dual numbers, and the four steps backward, by hand, and the two meet in the middle.

The objective

The parameters are

\[θ = \left(\vec{r},\ \vec{η},\ a_1, …, a_{(ℓ_α+1)^2}\right):\]

a rotation vector $\vec{r}$, a rapidity vector $\vec{η}$, and the real degrees of freedom of the supertranslation's modes. bms_from_parameters turns them into the boost velocity, the frame rotation, and the supertranslation that transform! takes. We write $P$ for the number of parameters.

The objective is

\[Φ(θ) = \frac{1}{𝒩} \sum_j w_j \sum_i v_i \sum_k u_k \left| μ^{ki}_{j}(θ) - c^{ki}_{j} \right|^2,\]

where

  • $j$ labels the pixels, which are the points of a grid of directions on the sphere of the new frame, and the $w_j$ are weights for integrating over that sphere;
  • $i$ labels the output times $t'_i$, and the $v_i$ are weights for integrating over them;
  • $k$ labels the components of the data, such as the strain and $ψ_4$, and the $u_k$ weight them against each other;
  • $μ^{ki}_{j}(θ)$ is the transformed waveform: its component $k$ at pixel $j$ and time $t'_i$;
  • $c^{ki}_{j}$ is the waveform that it is compared with, at the same pixels and times; and
  • $𝒩$ is a normalization, either 1 or the same sum with $μ$ set to zero.

So $Φ$ is a weighted and squared $L^2$ distance between the two waveforms, over the sphere and the output times. Everything in it except $μ$ is fixed before the minimization starts, and is stored in an ObjectivePlan, whose docstring describes the grid, the weights, and the choices that the caller has.

Each pixel contributes to $Φ$ on its own:

\[Φ = \frac{1}{𝒩} \sum_j ν_j, \qquad ν_j = \sum_{i,k} W_{jki} \left| μ^{ki}_{j} - c^{ki}_{j} \right|^2, \qquad W_{jki} = w_j\, v_i\, u_k.\]

The computation of $ν_j$ involves no pixel but $j$. So the pixel index passes untouched through everything that follows, until the sum over pixels at the very end, and we can differentiate one pixel at a time and add the results.

Indices are placed as in general relativity. An upper index is a slot of a vector, and a lower index is a slot of a covector; an upper index in the denominator of a derivative counts as a lower index. An index that appears once up and once down in a product is summed, and every other sum is written out.

The pixel index $j$ is only a label. It is always written as the first lower index of whatever belongs to a pixel, and it is never summed except by the $\sum_j$ above. The weights $W_{jki}$ and the times $t_h$, $t'_i$, and $τ_{ji}$ are fixed arrays of numbers, and all of their indices are labels of the same kind.

The four stages

The transformed waveform at pixel $j$ is computed from the modes of the original waveform in three stages, and a fourth compares the result with $c$. We name the stages $σ$, $ι$, $μ$, and $ν$ — for synthesis, interpolation, mixing, and norm — and we use the same letter, with indices, for the output of each stage. (On this page, $σ$ never denotes the shear.) The first three answer, in turn, where, when, and in which basis the original waveform is to be evaluated.

The input is the array $d^{kh}{}_{ℓm}$: mode $(ℓ,m)$ of component $k$ of the original waveform at the input time $t_h$.

Synthesis, $σ$. Because of the boost and the rotation, a direction in the new frame corresponds to a different direction in the original frame. The first stage evaluates the original waveform in that direction, at each of its own time samples:

\[σ^{kh}_{j} = d^{kh}{}_{ℓm}\ {}_{s_k}Y^{ℓm}_{j}.\]

Here ${}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}$ stands for the spin-weighted spherical harmonic evaluated at the rotor of pixel $j$ in the original frame, which aberration computes, and the sum runs over the modes with $ℓ ≥ |s_k|$. The harmonics are functions of the rotor, and not only of the direction, so this stage also accounts for the rotation of the dyad.

We usually think of such a sum as a linear map applied to the modes, with the harmonics as its coefficients. Here the roles are reversed. The modes are fixed data, and the harmonics are what depends on $θ$. So we regard $σ$ as a linear map applied to the harmonics, with the data as its coefficients, and the indices are placed accordingly.

Interpolation, $ι$. At pixel $j$, the output time $t'_i$ corresponds to the original time

\[τ_{ji} = κ_j^{-1}\, t'_i + α_j,\]

where $κ_j$ is the conformal factor and $α_j$ is the supertranslation, both evaluated at that pixel. This is the law $t' = κ(t - α)$ solved for $t$, with any sign convention for $α$ already applied. The second stage interpolates each component to those times, with the natural cubic spline through its samples:

\[ι^{ki}_{j} = S_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}\ σ^{kh}_{j}.\]

The value of a spline at any time is a linear combination of the samples it passes through, and $S_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}$ is the coefficient of the sample at $t_h$ in the value at $τ_{ji}$. These coefficients are real. They depend on the times, but not on the samples.

Mixing, $μ$. The components are defined relative to a tetrad, and the tetrad of the new frame differs from the original one by a rescaling and a null rotation. So each new component is a combination of the original ones, and the shear and the strain are also shifted by a multiple of $ð^2α$:

\[μ^{ki}_{j} = M_{j}{}^{k}{}_{k'}(τ_{ji})\ ι^{k'i}_{j} + q^{k}_{j}.\]

These are the transformation laws of the page BMS Action on Fields, which mix_components! applies. For example, if the components are the strain, $ψ_3$, and $ψ_4$ at $ℐ^+$, in that order and in the default conventions, then

\[\begin{aligned} μ^{1i}_{j} &= κ_j^{-1} \left( ι^{1i}_{j} - \bar{ð}^2 α_j \right), \\ μ^{2i}_{j} &= κ_j^{-3} \left( ι^{2i}_{j} + b_j(τ_{ji})\, ι^{3i}_{j} \right), \\ μ^{3i}_{j} &= κ_j^{-3}\, ι^{3i}_{j}, \end{aligned}\]

from which $M_{j}{}^{k}{}_{k'}$ and $q^{k}_{j}$ can be read off. The null-rotation parameter $b = ðt'/2κ$ is linear in the original time,

\[b_j(τ) = b^{(0)}_{j} + b^{(1)}_{j}\, τ,\]

which is why $M_{j}{}^{k}{}_{k'}$ depends on $τ_{ji}$; see Computing $ðt'/2κ$.

Norm, $ν$. The last stage compares the result with $c$ and reduces everything at the pixel to one real number:

\[ν_j = \sum_{i,k} W_{jki} \left| μ^{ki}_{j} - c^{ki}_{j} \right|^2.\]

Each stage acts on one kind of index and passes the others along untouched, and the pixel index passes through all four:

Where the parameters enter

The data, the times, the weights, and $c$ do not depend on $θ$. The parameters enter only through the geometry of each pixel, which consists of two sets of numbers:

  • the harmonics ${}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}$, for each spin weight $s$ among the components, which enter $σ$; and

  • eight real numbers,

    \[\left(g^1_j, …, g^8_j\right) = \left( κ_j^{-1},\ α_j,\ \operatorname{Re} b^{(0)}_{j},\ \operatorname{Im} b^{(0)}_{j},\ \operatorname{Re} b^{(1)}_{j},\ \operatorname{Im} b^{(1)}_{j},\ \operatorname{Re} ð^2α_j,\ \operatorname{Im} ð^2α_j \right),\]

    which enter $ι$ through the times $τ_{ji}$, and $μ$ through $M_{j}{}^{k}{}_{k'}$ and $q^{k}_{j}$.

So, for each pixel,

\[ν_j = ν\Bigl(μ\bigl(ι\left(σ(Y_j),\, g_j\right),\, g_j\bigr)\Bigr),\]

with $Y_j$ and $g_j$ functions of $θ$.

Where the work is

A waveform has hundreds of modes and thousands of time samples, or more of each. The synthesis is a sum over every mode, for every time sample of every component, which is $N_\mathrm{comp}\, N_t\, N_\mathrm{modes}$ multiplications for each pixel. For all but the smallest band limits, that is much more work than everything else together. The other stages never touch the modes: the interpolation costs a few operations for each input and output time, and the mixing and the norm cost a few for each output time. The geometry never touches the times: it costs a few operations for each mode. So the picture to keep in mind is of one expensive sum, $σ$, with cheap computations on either side of it.

The chain rule, multiplied in two orders

Everything on this page follows from the chain rule, together with one observation about the order in which its factors are multiplied. Suppose that a real number $ν$ is computed from parameters $θ^p$ in stages: first some quantities $a^A$ are computed from the $θ^p$, then some other quantities $b^B$ from the $a^A$, and then $ν$ from the $b^B$. The chain rule says that

\[\frac{∂ν}{∂θ^p} = \frac{∂ν}{∂b^B}\, \frac{∂b^B}{∂a^A}\, \frac{∂a^A}{∂θ^p}.\]

The right-hand side is a product of three arrays — the Jacobians of the three stages — with sums over $A$ and $B$. The product may be multiplied out starting from either end. The answer is the same, but the work is not, and the two choices are the two "modes" of automatic differentiation.

Forward mode starts at the right, with the parameters. It computes $∂a^A/∂θ^p$ along with $a^A$, then

\[\frac{∂b^B}{∂θ^p} = \frac{∂b^B}{∂a^A}\, \frac{∂a^A}{∂θ^p}\]

along with $b^B$, and so on to the end. At every stage it holds the derivatives of the current quantities with respect to all $P$ parameters. In geometric terms, it pushes the $P$ coordinate vectors $∂/∂θ^p$ forward through each map. This is what dual numbers do: every number is stored together with its $P$ derivatives, and every arithmetic operation updates both. Nothing has to be remembered from one stage to the next, but all the work of every stage is done $P + 1$ times.

Reverse mode starts at the left, with the result. It computes the covector $∂ν/∂b^B$, then

\[\frac{∂ν}{∂a^A} = \frac{∂ν}{∂b^B}\, \frac{∂b^B}{∂a^A},\]

and so on back to the beginning. At every stage it holds the derivatives of the one number $ν$ with respect to the current quantities. In geometric terms, it pulls the gradient $dν$ back through each map. There is one covector, however many parameters there are, so the work of every stage is done roughly twice: once to evaluate it, and once to pull the covector back through it. The price is paid in bookkeeping. The Jacobians are needed in the opposite order from the one in which the evaluation produces them, so the whole evaluation must be finished, and enough of it remembered, before the backward pass can start. A general-purpose tool has to record every operation as it goes.

Forward mode suits a computation with few inputs, then, and reverse mode suits one with few outputs. But nothing forces us to use one mode throughout. The product can be cut at any intermediate quantity, say $a^A$: we push the $P$ vectors forward as far as $∂a^A/∂θ^p$, pull the covector back as far as $∂ν/∂a^A$, and contract the two there.

Other names for these things

In the literature on automatic differentiation, $∂a^A/∂θ^p$ is called a tangent, and the step that advances it is called a pushforward or a Jacobian–vector product. The covector $∂ν/∂a^A$ is called a cotangent or an adjoint, and the step that takes it back is called a pullback or a vector–Jacobian product.

Derivatives with respect to complex quantities

One wrinkle remains. The parameters are real, and so is each $ν_j$, but most of the quantities between them are complex. We handle a complex quantity $z$ as we would a complex scalar field in a Lagrangian, by treating $z$ and $\bar{z}$ as independent variables, with

\[\frac{∂}{∂z} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{∂}{∂(\operatorname{Re} z)} - \mathrm{i}\, \frac{∂}{∂(\operatorname{Im} z)} \right), \qquad \frac{∂}{∂\bar{z}} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{∂}{∂(\operatorname{Re} z)} + \mathrm{i}\, \frac{∂}{∂(\operatorname{Im} z)} \right).\]

Three facts are all that we need, in which $ν$ stands for any real quantity.

  1. Because $ν$ is real, $∂ν/∂\bar{z}$ is the conjugate of $∂ν/∂z$. So the one complex number $∂ν/∂z$ holds everything about how $ν$ responds to $z$, and for complex quantities $z^a$

    \[dν = \frac{∂ν}{∂z^a}\, dz^a + \frac{∂ν}{∂\bar{z}^a}\, d\bar{z}^a = 2 \operatorname{Re} \left[ \frac{∂ν}{∂z^a}\, dz^a \right].\]

  2. If quantities $ζ^b$ are holomorphic functions of the $z^a$ — depending on the $z^a$, but not on their conjugates — then the chain rule has its ordinary form, with no conjugates anywhere:

    \[\frac{∂ν}{∂z^a} = \frac{∂ν}{∂ζ^b}\, \frac{∂ζ^b}{∂z^a}.\]

    The stages $σ$, $ι$, and $μ$ are of this kind.

  3. If the $z^a$ depend on a real quantity $x$, then by the first fact

    \[\frac{∂ν}{∂x} = 2 \operatorname{Re} \left[ \frac{∂ν}{∂z^a}\, \frac{∂z^a}{∂x} \right].\]

    This is how the real parameters will meet the complex harmonics.

A conjugate appears only where a function is not holomorphic, and here the only such function is the last one, $ν$.

The same thing in ChainRules

Julia's ChainRules represents the same information by the conjugate of twice our derivative, $∂ν/∂(\operatorname{Re} z) + \mathrm{i}\, ∂ν/∂(\operatorname{Im} z)$. With that choice, the backward step through multiplication by a complex number $A$ is multiplication by $\bar{A}$. With ours it is multiplication by $A$ itself, so that every backward step below uses the same array as its forward step, with no conjugation.

Forward through the geometry, backward through the data

For each pixel, the chain from the parameters to $ν_j$ is

\[θ^p \ \longmapsto\ \left( {}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j},\ g^n_j \right) \ \longmapsto\ σ^{kh}_{j} \ \longmapsto\ ι^{ki}_{j} \ \longmapsto\ μ^{ki}_{j} \ \longmapsto\ ν_j,\]

where the $g^n_j$ skip the synthesis and enter $ι$ and $μ$ directly. We have to decide where to cut it.

Forward mode all the way is what ForwardDiff.gradient does when it is applied to transform_objective. Each harmonic then has $P$ derivatives attached to it, and the synthesis has to be done for the values and again for each derivative: $P + 1$ syntheses for each pixel.

Reverse mode all the way would need only two sums of that size for each pixel: the synthesis, and one more to pull the covector back through it. But it would also need the backward pass of the geometry: of the products of rotors, the recursion for the harmonics, the supertranslation and its derivatives, and the null-rotation parameter. Written by hand, that would be long and delicate, and it would have to be rewritten whenever the code for the geometry changed.

Fortunately, it hardly matters how the geometry is differentiated. Nothing in it involves the time samples: the geometry of a pixel is computed once, however long the waveform is, and so are its derivatives. Forward mode costs $P$ times very little, where reverse mode would cost a few times very little, and dual numbers do it with no effort on our part. What does matter is the direction in which we pass through the synthesis: one covector must go back through it, rather than $P$ vectors forward. So we cut the chain at the geometry. The gradient of one pixel's $ν_j$ is then

\[\frac{∂ν_j}{∂θ^p} = 2 \operatorname{Re} \left[ \sum_s \frac{∂ν_j}{∂\,{}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}}\, \frac{∂\,{}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}}{∂θ^p} \right] + \frac{∂ν_j}{∂g^n_j}\, \frac{∂g^n_j}{∂θ^p},\]

where the sum over $s$ runs over the spin weights among the components. In each product, the first factor is a covector that has been pulled back from $ν_j$, and the second is one of the $P$ vectors that have been pushed forward from the parameters. So there are three things to compute:

  1. $∂\,{}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}/∂θ^p$ and $∂g^n_j/∂θ^p$, by forward mode through the geometry, with dual numbers;
  2. $∂ν_j/∂\,{}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}$, by a backward pass through $ν$, $μ$, $ι$, and $σ$, written by hand; and
  3. $∂ν_j/∂g^n_j$, which is only eight numbers, by a combination of the two.

For example, take $ℓ_\mathrm{max} = 16$, so that there are 289 modes, with 4000 time samples, two components, and a supertranslation up to $ℓ_α = 4$, so that $P = 31$. One synthesis is then about 2.3 million multiplications for each pixel. Forward mode all the way does 32 of them. The cut chain does two, plus work that involves no sum of that size.

The backward pass is easy to write by hand because the stages are so simple. The stages $σ$ and $ι$ are linear in what flows through them, and $μ$ is linear up to an offset. So their Jacobians are just the arrays that the stages themselves apply:

\[\frac{∂σ^{kh}_{j}}{∂\,{}_{s_k}Y^{ℓm}_{j}} = d^{kh}{}_{ℓm}, \qquad \frac{∂ι^{ki}_{j}}{∂σ^{k'h}_{j}} = δ^{k}{}_{k'}\, S_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}, \qquad \frac{∂μ^{ki}_{j}}{∂ι^{k'i'}_{j}} = M_{j}{}^{k}{}_{k'}(τ_{ji})\, δ^{i}{}_{i'}.\]

None of these depends on the values that flow through the stages; they depend only on the data and on the $g^n_j$. So, unlike a general computation in reverse mode, ours has nothing to record on the way forward except its final result, the residual $μ^{ki}_{j} - c^{ki}_{j}$.

The backward pass

Each step takes the derivatives of $ν_j$ with respect to the output of a stage, and returns its derivatives with respect to the input of that stage.

Through $ν$. Writing $|z|^2 = z \bar{z}$ and differentiating with $\bar{z}$ held fixed,

\[\frac{∂ν_j}{∂μ^{ki}_{j}} = W_{jki} \left( \bar{μ}^{ki}_{j} - \bar{c}^{ki}_{j} \right),\]

with no sum, because the indices of the weights are labels. This is the only conjugate in the backward pass. The weights act as a metric: they turn the conjugated residual, which is a vector, into a covector.

Through $μ$.

\[\frac{∂ν_j}{∂ι^{ki}_{j}} = \frac{∂ν_j}{∂μ^{k'i}_{j}}\, M_{j}{}^{k'}{}_{k}(τ_{ji}).\]

The mixing acts on the component index and passes the time index along, and so does this step: for each output time, the same coefficients are summed over their other index. The offset $q^{k}_{j}$ does not depend on $ι$, so it drops out here. Its dependence on the geometry is not lost; it enters through $∂ν_j/∂g^n_j$, below.

Through $ι$.

\[\frac{∂ν_j}{∂σ^{kh}_{j}} = \frac{∂ν_j}{∂ι^{ki}_{j}}\, S_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}.\]

The interpolation acts on the time index and passes the component index along. The array $S_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}$ is never formed. It would be full, since every sample influences the spline everywhere, but the interpolation applies it in a number of operations proportional to $N_t + N_{t'}$, by solving a tridiagonal system and then evaluating a cubic on each interval. The sum over $i$ here is done by the same operations in the opposite order, at the same cost; spline_transpose! does it.

How the spline is run backward

Consider one component, and drop its index $k$. Suppose that $t_{h_0} ≤ τ_{ji} < t_{h_0+1}$. The value of the spline at $τ_{ji}$ is a combination of the two samples $σ^{h_0}_{j}$ and $σ^{h_0+1}_{j}$, and of the second derivatives $\ddot{σ}^{h_0}_{j}$ and $\ddot{σ}^{h_0+1}_{j}$ of the spline at those two times:

\[ι^{i}_{j} = E_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}\ σ^{h}_{j} + F_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}\ \ddot{σ}^{h}_{j},\]

where $E_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}$ and $F_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}$ are nonzero only for $h = h_0$ and $h = h_0 + 1$. The second derivatives vanish at the first and last times, which is what makes the spline "natural", and at the times between they solve a tridiagonal system,

\[Q^{h}{}_{h'}\, \ddot{σ}^{h'}_{j} = B^{h}{}_{h'}\, σ^{h'}_{j},\]

where $B^{h}{}_{h'}$ takes second differences of the samples. Neither $Q$ nor $B$ depends on the pixel. So

\[S_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h} = E_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h} + F_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h'} \left(Q^{-1}\right)^{h'}{}_{h''} B^{h''}{}_{h},\]

and the backward step is

\[\frac{∂ν_j}{∂σ^{h}_{j}} = \frac{∂ν_j}{∂ι^{i}_{j}}\, E_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h} + ζ_{jh''}\, B^{h''}{}_{h}, \qquad \text{where} \qquad ζ_{jh''}\, Q^{h''}{}_{h'} = \frac{∂ν_j}{∂ι^{i}_{j}}\, F_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h'}.\]

In words: each $∂ν_j/∂ι^{i}_{j}$ is spread onto the two samples and the two second derivatives on either side of $τ_{ji}$; the part on the second derivatives goes through a tridiagonal solve; and the result is spread onto the samples by the second differences. The matrix of the coefficients $Q^{h}{}_{h'}$ is symmetric. So the system for $ζ$, in which $Q$ is contracted on its other index, has the same matrix as the forward solve, and reuses its factors.

spline_transpose! takes $E$ and $F$ from the same evaluation function as the forward interpolation, and chooses the interval of each $τ_{ji}$ in the same way, including for a $τ_{ji}$ that lands on a sample or, by roundoff, a hair outside the first or last one. So the two agree to roundoff.

Through $σ$.

\[\frac{∂ν_j}{∂\,{}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}} = \sum_{k:\, s_k = s} \frac{∂ν_j}{∂σ^{kh}_{j}}\, d^{kh}{}_{ℓm}.\]

The sum over $k$ collects the components that share the spin weight $s$, and so share their harmonics. The synthesis summed the data over the modes, for each time and component; this step sums the same data over the times and components, for each mode. It is the same number of multiplications, $N_\mathrm{comp}\, N_t\, N_\mathrm{modes}$, and it is the second of the two expensive operations, and the last.

The forward passes

The geometry, with $P$ derivatives. Each parameter is made into a dual number that holds the value $θ^p$ and the derivatives $∂θ^p/∂θ^q = δ^p{}_q$. The same functions that compute the geometry for the objective then return it together with its derivatives: bms_from_parameters; aberration, for each pixel's rotor in the original frame; supertranslation_on_grid; compute_ðt′╱2κ; the conformal factor; and the calculator for the harmonics, from SphericalFunctions. This gives $∂\,{}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}/∂θ^p$ and $∂g^n_j/∂θ^p$.

Eight parameters at a time

A dual number that held all $P$ derivatives would have a different type for each $P$, and the code would have to be compiled again for every new number of parameters. So the dual numbers hold eight derivatives, and the geometry is computed once for each group of eight parameters, with any places to spare in the last group left at zero. The derivatives that come out are the same. The extra work is that the values of the geometry are computed again for each group, which costs about as much as a few more derivatives.

Note that a quantity may depend on the parameters in more than one way. For example, $ð^2α_j$ changes with the modes of the supertranslation, and also with the direction at which it is evaluated, which moves with the boost and the rotation. Dual numbers account for every such path without our having to list them, which is exactly why we use them here.

The eight numbers. We still need $∂ν_j/∂g^n_j$. By the third fact about complex quantities,

\[\frac{∂ν_j}{∂g^n_j} = 2 \operatorname{Re} \left[ \frac{∂ν_j}{∂μ^{ki}_{j}}\, \left. \frac{∂μ^{ki}_{j}}{∂g^n_j} \right|_{σ} \right],\]

where the derivative of $μ$ is taken through both $ι$ and $μ$, with the synthesized values $σ^{kh}_{j}$ held fixed. The eight numbers enter in three ways: $κ_j^{-1}$ and $α_j$ move the times $τ_{ji}$ at which the spline is evaluated; $κ_j^{-1}$, $b^{(0)}_{j}$, and $b^{(1)}_{j}$ set the coefficients $M_{j}{}^{k}{}_{k'}$, which also depend on $τ_{ji}$; and $κ_j^{-1}$ and $ð^2α_j$ set the offsets $q^{k}_{j}$. We could differentiate each of these by hand. But there are only eight directions, so forward mode is cheap here too: $ι$ and $μ$ are evaluated with dual numbers that hold derivatives with respect to the eight $g^n_j$, which gives $μ^{ki}_{j}$ and $∂μ^{ki}_{j}/∂g^n_j$ together. This costs several times as much as $ι$ and $μ$ themselves, but it involves no sum over modes, and it is the same whatever the number of parameters.

Assembling the gradient

Summing over the pixels,

\[\frac{∂Φ}{∂θ^p} = \frac{1}{𝒩} \sum_j \frac{∂ν_j}{∂θ^p} = \frac{1}{𝒩} \sum_j \left\{ 2 \operatorname{Re} \left[ \sum_s \frac{∂ν_j}{∂\,{}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}}\, \frac{∂\,{}_{s}Y^{ℓm}_{j}}{∂θ^p} \right] + \frac{∂ν_j}{∂g^n_j}\, \frac{∂g^n_j}{∂θ^p} \right\}.\]

Each pixel's term is complete in itself: its $ν_j$ is evaluated, the covector is pulled back to its harmonics, and the contraction with its $P$ vectors is done, all without reference to any other pixel. So nothing has to be stored for the whole grid at once, and the pixels can be shared among threads.

The diagram shows the whole computation for one pixel. The evaluation runs from left to right along the top, and the backward pass returns from right to left below it. The derivatives of the geometry with respect to the parameters come down the left side, and meet the covectors at the bottom.

Comparing only the modes up to $ℓ_\mathrm{fit}$

An ObjectivePlan built with ℓ_fit = L compares only the modes with $ℓ ≤ L$ of the two waveforms. This is the usual way to compare waveforms that were padded to a higher band limit to make room for the transformation, because it leaves out the modes that were added, and it also leaves out noise at higher $ℓ$. The residual is projected onto the modes with $ℓ ≤ L$ by the quadrature,

\[Π^{ki}{}_{ℓm} = \sum_j w_j\ \overline{{}_{s_k}Y'^{ℓm}_{j}}\, \left( μ^{ki}_{j} - c^{ki}_{j} \right),\]

where ${}_{s}Y'^{ℓm}_{j}$ is the harmonic evaluated at pixel $j$ of the grid itself, in the new frame, and so does not depend on $θ$. The objective is then the weighted sum of squares of these modes:

\[Φ = \frac{1}{𝒩} \sum_{i,k} v_i\, u_k \sum_{ℓm:\ |s_k| ≤ ℓ ≤ L} \left| Π^{ki}{}_{ℓm} \right|^2.\]

This is no longer a sum of terms that each belong to one pixel. But it depends on the transformed waveform only through $Π$, which is linear in it, so the facts above give

\[𝒩\, \frac{∂Φ}{∂μ^{ki}_{j}} = W_{jki}\ \overline{\,\sum_{ℓm} Π^{ki}{}_{ℓm}\ {}_{s_k}Y'^{ℓm}_{j}\,}.\]

That is the formula for $∂ν_j/∂μ^{ki}_{j}$ again, with the residual replaced by its projection onto the modes with $ℓ ≤ L$, evaluated back at the pixel. The backward pass for each pixel starts from this covector, and nothing else changes. Because $Π$ is needed before any pixel can start, a first pass over all the pixels computes it, at the cost of one more evaluation of the objective.

Cost and memory

For each pixel, the objective costs one synthesis, and the gradient costs two sums of that size: the synthesis, and the backward step through it. Nothing else in the gradient involves a sum over the modes and the times together: not the interpolation and the mixing with their eight derivatives, nor the backward steps through them, nor the geometry with its $P$ derivatives. Only the last of these grows with the number of parameters. For each pixel it takes a number of operations proportional to $P\, N_\mathrm{modes}$, mostly for the derivatives of the harmonics, where a synthesis takes $N_\mathrm{comp}\, N_t\, N_\mathrm{modes}$. So its share is proportional to $P / (N_\mathrm{comp}\, N_t)$, which is small whenever the waveform has many more time samples than the transformation has parameters.

Both the objective and the gradient take the pixels a block at a time, so that the synthesis of a block — and, in the gradient, the backward step through it — is one matrix product for each component.

A block holds as many pixels as fit in the memory that the data themselves occupy, or in 128 MiB if that is larger, but at least four for each thread. The harmonics of each pixel are evaluated first as plain numbers, for the synthesis, and again with their derivatives, for the contraction, because keeping the derivatives for a whole block would take $P + 1$ times the memory. A general-purpose tool for reverse mode would instead have to record every operation of every synthesis, of which there are $N_\mathrm{pix}\, N_\mathrm{comp}\, N_t\, N_\mathrm{modes}$.

Against finite differences

Before transform_objective existed, the only way to get a gradient was by finite differences through transform!. That transformation chooses its own output times, which move with the parameters, as does the one in the Python package scri. So each evaluation of an objective is a transformation, an interpolation onto the times of the waveform it is compared with, and a sum of squares, and a gradient by forward differences is $P + 1$ of those.

How it is checked

The tests in test/gradient.jl check each piece, and the whole.

  • The backward step through $ι$ is checked against the forward interpolation: for random complex $X_i$ and $Z^h$, the number $X_i\, S_{j}{}^{i}{}_{h}\, Z^{h}$ is computed once by interpolating $Z$ and once by applying spline_transpose! to $X$. This is done on unevenly spaced times, from four samples up, with output times on the samples and at the ends.
  • The backward step through $σ$ is checked against the synthesis in the same way, including for components that share a spin weight.
  • $M_{j}{}^{k}{}_{k'}$ and $q^{k}_{j}$, as mixing_matrix! extracts them, are checked against mix_components!, on both null infinities and in more than one convention.
  • The value and the gradient are checked against ForwardDiff applied to transform_objective: at the identity and away from it, for components that mix, on both null infinities, with weights, with normalization, and with $ℓ_\mathrm{fit}$. The two gradients agree to a relative error of about $10^{-15}$, and the same comparison passes in Double64 with a tolerance of $10^{-25}$.
  • The gradient is checked against central differences, which is independent of ForwardDiff.

The notation and the code
